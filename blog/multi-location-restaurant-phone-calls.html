Multi-Location Restaurant Phone Calls: How to Run One Standard Across Every Store (2026 Guide)

One store can run its phone on habit. The manager knows the regulars, the team knows the answers and problems get handled at the counter. At ten or fifty locations, habit stops working. Every store answers a little differently, complaints get lost between shifts, catering requests go to whoever picked up, and the people at corporate have almost no idea what happens on the phone at each location.

The phone is still where a large share of guest problems, orders and questions start. This guide is for operators, district managers and directors of operations at multi-unit restaurant groups. It covers why phones break down as you add locations, how to set one standard that still allows for local differences, who should get which call, how to handle complaints and team member calls, what to measure at each store and what to ask any AI phone agent vendor.

Quick Answer: How Should Multi-Location Restaurants Handle Phone Calls?

  1. Answer every call at every store, including at peak and after hours, with the same standard everywhere.
  2. Keep one set of answers for the whole group, with a short list of exceptions for each location.
  3. Decide who gets which call: orders, catering, complaints, managers and team members each have a destination.
  4. Turn every complaint into a ticket with the location, the guest's details and the full call, and close it.
  5. Report by store and by brand, and review it every week.

Why the Phone Breaks Down as You Add Locations

  • Every store gives a different answer. One location says the patio is dog-friendly, another says it isn't. One quotes 20 minutes for pickup, another quotes 40. Guests notice, and it chips away at the brand.
  • Updates don't reach every store. Holiday hours, a new menu item or a closed location: corporate sends the update, and some stores are still giving the old answer two weeks later.
  • The right person never hears about it. A catering lead, a food safety complaint or a request for the GM gets written on a sticky note, if it gets written down at all.
  • Complaints disappear. A guest calls about a missing item, the shift lead promises a callback and then the shift ends. Nobody at corporate knows the call happened.
  • Team member calls tie up the guest line. Questions about schedules, pay and time off land on the same phone guests use to order.
  • Brands run on different systems. Groups that grew by acquisition often have one brand on Toast, another on Aloha and a third ordering through Olo, which makes one consistent phone process harder.
  • Nobody can see it. Most groups can tell you sales by store to the dollar, but not how many calls each store missed last Saturday or why people called.

Step 1: Write One Set of Answers, With Local Exceptions

Start with the questions guests actually ask. Pull a week of calls from a few stores, or ask your GMs to list the ten questions they hear most. Then split the answers into two groups:

  • Group-wide answers: menu and ingredients, allergens, gift cards, the loyalty program, catering packages, return and refund policy, how online ordering works.
  • Location-specific answers: address and directions, hours and holiday hours, parking, delivery area, patio and private dining, accessibility, anything only that store offers.

Keep both in one place that someone owns. When an answer changes, it should change once, for every store at the same time, and not depend on fifty managers reading the same email. This is also the document you'll give any phone agent you evaluate, so it's worth doing well.

Step 2: Decide Who Gets Which Call

Most calls a restaurant gets fall into a short list. Decide where each type goes, for every location, before the next busy week:

  • Questions: answered on the call, from the shared answers above.
  • Takeout orders: taken on the call and sent into that store's POS, or a text to the guest with a link to your online ordering.
  • Catering and large orders: to the store manager or a central catering team, depending on who fulfills them. Our guide to catering calls covers what to capture.
  • Complaints: to the store's GM in writing, with a copy to the district manager for serious issues.
  • A guest who asks for a person: transferred to the store, with a fallback when nobody picks up.
  • Team members: to HR or a help desk, not the line staff.
  • Vendors and sales calls: to an email address, not a live transfer during the rush.
  • Spam and robocalls: blocked.

Write down who covers each one when that person is off. The routing that fails is usually the one built around a single manager who happens to be on vacation.

Step 3: Treat Every Complaint as a Ticket

A guest who calls to complain is giving you a chance to keep them. Most groups lose that chance not because the GM doesn't care, but because the complaint never reaches the GM in a usable form. Every complaint call should produce a written record with:

  • The location, date and time of the visit or order
  • The guest's name and phone number
  • What happened, in the guest's words, ideally with the full call transcript
  • What the guest is asking for: a refund, a remake, a callback or just to be heard
  • Who owns the follow-up, and by when

Then close the loop. Review open complaints by store every week, and look for patterns: one location with a run of missing-item calls usually has a packing problem, not a phone problem.

Step 4: Keep Team Member Calls Off the Guest Line

At scale, a surprising share of calls to a store aren't from guests at all. Team members call about schedules, pay stubs, time off and HR questions, and former employees call about W-2s. Each of those calls pulls a manager off the floor, and many of them can't be answered at the store anyway.

Give those calls their own path: a help desk ticket, an HR inbox or a direct transfer to the right person. Your store teams get time back, and team members get answers from someone who can actually help. Privacy requests from guests, such as CCPA requests to access or delete their data, belong on a tracked path like this too.

Step 5: Measure Every Store the Same Way

You can't run a standard you can't see. Track these for each location, and compare stores side by side:

  • Call volume by hour and by day
  • Why people called: orders, questions, catering, complaints, reservations
  • How many calls turned into orders, and the revenue from them
  • How many calls were transferred to the store, and how many of those were answered
  • Complaints by type, and how fast each one was closed
  • Missed calls, if any still get missed

A Weekly Phone Review for District Managers

None of this works unless someone looks at it. A short weekly routine is enough:

  1. Check call volume and missed calls for each store in your district.
  2. Read the transcripts of every complaint and every call that was transferred.
  3. Make sure every open complaint has an owner and a date, and close the ones that are done.
  4. Note any question the phone got wrong or couldn't answer, and fix it in the shared answers.
  5. Update anything coming up: holiday hours, a closure, a new item or a promotion.

Thirty minutes on a Monday is usually enough to catch the problems that otherwise show up in online reviews.

When Your Brands Use Different POS Systems

Many groups run more than one POS, especially after an acquisition or when they own several brands. That shouldn't mean several phone processes. When you evaluate a phone agent, check that it connects to every POS you run, takes orders into each one directly and still gives you one set of reporting across all of them. Questions to Ask Any AI Phone Agent Vendor About Multiple Locations

Whether you're comparing HeyTARA, Loman AI, Palona AI, SoundHound, Slang AI, ConverseNow or anyone else, ask:

  1. Can we keep one set of answers for the group and override hours, holiday hours and other details for each store?

  3. Where do complaints go, and does our team get the full call transcript with each one?
  4. Can team member and HR calls go to our help desk instead of the store?
  5. Does the dashboard show every location side by side, and by brand?
  6. Does it connect to every POS we run, and take orders into each one directly?
  7. Who sets up each location, and who do we call when something needs to change?

The Bottom Line

At one store, the phone is a habit. Across many, it needs a system: one set of answers with local exceptions, a clear destination for every type of call, complaints handled as tickets, team member calls kept off the guest line and the same report for every store, reviewed every week. Get that right and the phone stops being the place where your standard slips.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do multi-location restaurants keep phone answers consistent?

Keep one master set of answers for the whole group, with a short list of exceptions for each store, such as hours, holiday hours, parking and delivery area. Whoever answers the phone, a person or an AI phone agent, should work from that same source, and someone should own keeping it up to date.

Who should get a complaint call at a multi-unit restaurant group?

Usually the general manager of the store involved, with the district manager copied for serious issues. The complaint should arrive in writing with the location, the guest's contact details and what happened, so nothing depends on a message passed along at shift change. On HeyTARA's Main Course plan, complaint details, the location and the full call transcript are emailed to your team for follow-up.

Should employee calls go through the same line as guest calls?

It's better if they don't tie up the people serving guests. Team members calling about schedules, pay or HR questions can be sent to a help desk or HR contact instead. On HeyTARA's Chef's Special plan, team member and HR inquiries are routed to your help desk, such as Zendesk.

What phone metrics should a restaurant group track by location?

At minimum: call volume by hour and day, why people called, how many calls turned into orders, how many were transferred, how many complaints came in and how fast each was closed, and how many calls were missed. Compare stores side by side every week rather than only looking at group totals.

Can one AI phone agent work if our brands use different POS systems?

It can if the vendor connects to all of them. HeyTARA connects to Toast, Clover, Square, SpotOn, NCR Aloha, SkyTab and any POS that uses Olo, so groups with different systems across brands or locations can run one phone agent with one set of reporting.
