Holiday Party Inquiries: How Restaurants Can Book More Private Events in December (2026 Guide)

December is private event season. Office holiday parties, team dinners, family get-togethers, a 40th birthday that happens to fall on the second Saturday of the month. For a restaurant with a private room or a space it can close off, a few good holiday bookings can make the month. And every one of them starts the same way: a guest fills out a form, sends an email, posts a question or picks up the phone.

What happens next decides most of the bookings. The guest planning the party is usually asking three or four places at once, often in between their actual job. The inquiry lands in a shared inbox during dinner service. Someone gets to it the next afternoon, asks for the date and headcount, waits for a reply, and by the time the conversation gets to menus, the guest has put a deposit down somewhere else. This guide covers how to turn more of those inquiries into bookings: what to set up before the first one arrives, how fast to reply, what to ask, how to follow up and how to hand your events team a lead that's ready to book.

Quick Answer: How Do Restaurants Book More Holiday Parties?

  1. Have your event information ready before the inquiries start: spaces, capacities, minimums, menus, photos and open dates.
  2. Reply in minutes, not days, including at night and on weekends.
  3. Ask the qualifying questions up front, so your events team starts with the details instead of collecting them.
  4. Answer the common questions the same way every time, from information your team has approved.
  5. Follow up when guests go quiet, because a lot of them do.
  6. Hand your events team a ready lead, and call the guests who are ready to book first.
  7. Don't forget the phone, where many party inquiries still start.

Why Holiday Party Inquiries Get Lost

  • Guests shop around. The person planning the party usually asks several venues at the same time and leans toward the ones that answer first and clearly.
  • Inquiries arrive during service. Forms get filled out in the evening and on weekends, exactly when your managers are on the floor.
  • Nobody owns the inbox. A shared events inbox that everyone can see is often an inbox no one clears.
  • The first few messages are spent on basics. What date? How many people? What's the budget? Each round trip can take a day.
  • Guests go quiet. They get busy, they wait for their boss to approve a budget, they forget. A guest who doesn't answer the first reply rarely gets a second one.
  • Dates fill up. In December, a lead that waits two days may be asking about a Friday that's already gone.

Step 1: Know What You're Selling Before the First Inquiry

The fastest reply in the world doesn't help if nobody can answer the guest's questions. Before holiday season, put your event information in one place that your whole team, and anything that replies for you, works from:

  • Each space and its capacity: how many guests seated and standing, and whether the space is fully private or a section of the dining room.
  • Minimums and pricing: food and beverage minimums by day of the week, room fees, service charges and the deposit you require.
  • Event menus: prix fixe, family style, buffet and passed appetizers, with prices per person and drink packages.
  • Photos of each space, set up the way guests would see it for a party.
  • Dates that are open, held and booked, especially Thursdays, Fridays and Saturdays in December.
  • Policies: outside cakes and decorations, AV and music, how long guests can stay, cancellations and when the final headcount is due.

If you run more than one location, keep this information per location: each store has its own spaces, menus, minimums and managers. Our guide to running one standard across every store covers how to keep the shared parts consistent.

Step 2: Reply in Minutes, Not Days

Speed matters more for event inquiries than almost anything else a restaurant answers. The guest is comparing options right now, while the party is on their mind. A reply within a few minutes catches them while they're still at their desk or on their phone. A reply tomorrow afternoon competes with three other venues that may have already answered.

A fast first reply doesn't have to be the whole answer. It needs to do three things: thank the guest by name in your restaurant's name, show that a real conversation has started and ask the first question. Text often works better than email for this, because guests answer texts faster. Whatever you use, it has to work at 9 p.m. on a Friday and on Sunday morning, when a lot of party planning happens.

Step 3: Ask the Qualifying Questions Up Front

Your events coordinator's time is best spent selling the event, not collecting the date. Gather the basics in the first conversation:

  1. The date, and a backup date if the first one is taken
  2. Start time and how long the event will run
  3. Number of guests
  4. Type of event: an office party, a team dinner, a family celebration, a birthday
  5. Food and beverage preferences: seated dinner, buffet, appetizers only, open bar or drink tickets
  6. Budget, if the guest is willing to share one
  7. Dietary needs and allergies
  8. Special requests: AV for a toast or a slideshow, a cake, decorations, a quiet space for speeches
  9. Who is deciding, and by when

The last one is easy to skip and worth asking. A guest who needs an answer by Friday is a different lead from one who is just collecting quotes for next month.

Step 4: Answer the Common Questions the Same Way Every Time

Holiday party guests ask the same things over and over. Write the answers down once, get them approved and use them everywhere:

  • Is the space fully private? How many people does it fit?
  • What's the food and beverage minimum, and is there a room fee?
  • What menus do you offer, and how much are they per person?
  • Can we customize the menu or do a drink package?
  • How much is the deposit, and when is the final headcount due?
  • Can we bring our own cake or decorations?
  • Do you have a microphone, a screen or music?
  • Is there parking, and is the space accessible?

Anything that isn't on the list, like a custom price, a late night or an unusual setup, should go to your events team instead of getting a guess. A guest who is told one thing in the first message and something else at signing is a guest who doesn't come back.

Step 5: Follow Up When Guests Go Quiet

A lot of event inquiries stall after the first exchange, and it usually isn't because the guest decided against you. They got busy, they're waiting on a budget or a headcount or they're still hearing back from other places. A friendly check-in about a day later brings many of them back:

  • Keep it short, and give them something useful: the menu that fits their headcount, or a photo of the room.
  • If the date they asked for is filling up, say so. It's honest and it helps them decide.
  • If they say they've booked elsewhere, thank them and invite them back for the next one.

Step 6: Hand Your Events Team a Ready Lead

The goal of the first conversation is a lead your events team can act on in one read. That means one summary per guest, not a thread to scroll through:

  • The event details: date, time, headcount, event type, food and beverage, budget and special requests.
  • A short summary of the conversation so far.
  • Open questions that need someone from the team, like a custom price or a special arrangement.
  • How ready the guest is: who wants to move forward now and who is still comparing.

Then call the guests who are ready to book first. In December, an hour can be the difference between holding a Friday night and losing it. Your team still finalizes the menu, the contract, the deposit and the reservation in the systems you already use.

Step 7: Don't Forget the Inquiries That Come In by Phone

Plenty of party planners skip the form and call. If that call reaches a host in the middle of a rush, the details end up on a sticky note, or the guest is told to call back tomorrow when the manager is in. Treat a phone inquiry like a form inquiry: capture the same details, tell the guest when they'll hear back and send the lead to the person who manages events at that location. If the call is about food leaving the restaurant rather than a party in your space, it's a catering order, and our guide to restaurant catering calls covers that side.

Short on Time? Do These Five Things This Week

If you're reading this at the end of November with an events inbox that's already filling up:

  1. Clear the inbox today and reply to every open inquiry, even if it's only to ask whether they still need a space.
  2. Put one person in charge of event inquiries for each shift, with a goal for how fast they reply.
  3. Make a one-page sheet with your spaces, capacities, minimums and menu prices, so anyone can answer the basics.
  4. List the December dates that are still open, and say so in your replies.
  5. Add a few questions to your inquiry form: date, backup date, headcount, event type and budget.

How to Measure Your Event Inquiries

After the season, look at what happened while it's fresh:

  • How many inquiries came in, and from where: the website form, email, the phone, social media.
  • How long it took to send the first reply, on average and at night or on weekends.
  • How many inquiries turned into qualified leads, and how many turned into bookings.
  • Revenue from private events, by location and by space.
  • Why guests didn't book: the date was taken, the budget didn't fit, the space was too small or nobody followed up.

The last list tells you what to fix before next season, and whether the problem is your spaces and prices or how quickly you answered.

Questions to Ask Any Vendor That Follows Up on Event Leads

  1. How quickly does it reply after a guest submits our form, and does it work at night and on weekends?
  2. Does it reply in our restaurant's name, and can guests reply back?
  3. Does it answer only from information we approve? What happens when it doesn't know?
  4. Can it share our event menus and photos of our spaces?
  5. Does it follow up if the guest goes quiet?
  6. What does our events team get at the end, and how do we know who is ready to book?
  7. Can each location have its own spaces, menus and managers?
  8. Does it work with the inquiry form we already have, and does it also handle event inquiries by phone?

Frequently Asked Questions

When do guests start booking holiday parties at restaurants?

Office and group holiday parties are often planned weeks ahead, and many inquiries arrive in October and November for December dates. A second wave comes in late November and early December from groups that waited, and they usually need an answer fast because the open dates are going. Plan to answer inquiries quickly through the whole season, not just early on.

How fast should a restaurant reply to a private event inquiry?

As fast as you can, ideally within minutes. Guests planning a party often contact several venues at once, and the venue that replies first with clear answers has the best chance of getting the booking. A reply the next afternoon may arrive after the guest has already chosen somewhere else.

What should a restaurant ask in a private event inquiry?

The date and a backup date, start time, number of guests, type of event, food and beverage preferences, budget or minimum spend, dietary needs, any special requests like AV or a cake, and the best way to reach the guest. Ask who is making the decision and when they need to decide, so your events team knows how urgent the lead is.

Should restaurants reply to event inquiries by text or by email?

Many guests answer a text faster than an email, especially when they're planning a party between other things. Texting in the restaurant's name, with the guest's permission through your inquiry form, keeps the conversation moving. Send the full menus, photos and contract by whatever channel your events team uses to close.

What's the difference between catering and a private event?

Catering is food that leaves the restaurant, for an office lunch or a party at someone's home. A private event happens at your restaurant, in a private room, a section or the whole space. The questions are different: a private event needs a space, a date, a time and a minimum spend, so it helps to send those inquiries to whoever manages your event spaces.
